 Dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy

Introduction

Dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy, commonly known as DRPLA, is a progressive brain disorder with protean clinical manifestations consisting of various combinations of myoclonus, seizures, ataxia, choreoathetosis, and dementia. The clinical presentation correlates with the size of the causative CAG repeats, and as such, affected family members can present with very different patterns of the disorder.

This condition is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, which means one copy of the altered gene in each cell is enough to cause the disorder. In most cases, an affected person has one parent with the condition.

The average age of onset of DRPLA is 30 years, but this condition can appear anytime from infancy to mid-adulthood.

First Described

The first description of DRPLA, was by Titica and van Boegaert in 1946. They reported two cases in a single family with progressive choreoathetosis, ataxia, and dementia [1][1].

In 1958, an article was published presenting a case report of someone that suffered from this unknown disease at the time [2][2].

Epidemiology

DRPLA is most common in the Japanese population, where it has an estimated incidence of 2 to 7 per million people. This condition has also been seen in families from North America and Europe.

Although DRPLA is rare in the United States, it has been studied in a large African American family from the Haw River area of North Carolina. When the family was first identified, researchers named the disorder Haw River syndrome. Later, researchers determined that Haw River syndrome and DRPLA are the same condition [3][3].

Causative gene

DRPLA is caused by a mutation in the ATN1 gene. It has 10 exons from which 9 are coding exons (figure 2). ATN1 gene is located on chromosome 12 (12p.13.31) (figure 3).

The ATN1 mutation that underlies DRPLA involves the CAG trinucleotide repeats. Normally, this segment is repeated 6 to 35 times within the ATN1 gene. In people with DRPLA, the CAG segment is repeated at least 48 times, and the repeat region may be two or three times its usual length [3][3]. The abnormally long CAG trinucleotide repeat changes the structure of atrophin-1. This altered protein accumulates in neurons and interferes with normal cell functions.

Codified protein: structure, domains, functions and intracellular localization

Structure and domains

ATN1 gene encodes a protein called atrophin-1, a member of a class of evolutionarily conserved transcriptional corepressors involved in nuclear signalling (figure 4). Atrophin-1 (from transcript variant 1) has 1190 aminoacids with 125,414 Da.

Protein functions and biological role

Atrophin-1 has some important molecular and biological functions:

  • Corepressor activity towards transcription by recruiting NR2E1 and by being a corepressor of MTG8 [5][5].
  • Necessary for central nervous system development [6][6].
  • Involved in neuron apoptotic processes [7][7].

Pathophysiology

Dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy is one of the CAG repeat disorders that show atrophin-1 protein deposition in the nucleus of affected neurons, known as neuronal intranuclear inclusions (NIIs), but according to immunohistochemical studies on autopsied tissues of patients affected with DRPLA, there has been seen a more diffuse accumulation of mutant atrophin-1 rather than the NIIs [8][8].

Atrophin-1 is targeted by lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) which leads to neuronal pluripotent development of cells in the cortex. Mutated atrophin-1 is hardly destroyed by the cells, therefore more demethylation done by LSD1 will further increase the expression of atrophin-1. LSD1 has been investigated as a target to control atrophin-1 expression. With KO or knockdown LSD1, atrophin-1 suffers downregulation of its expression [9][9].

Another pathogenic mechanism regarding mutant atrophin-1 is its binding to TAFII130 which is a transcriptional activator. This binding leads to transcriptional dysregulation [10]. Also related to transcriptional activation mechanisms, CREB-dependent activation is tampered by expanded polyglutamine stretches from mutated atrophin-1 [10][10].

Clinical manifestations: neuropathology, other signs and symptoms

Neuropathology

The most common pathological evidences seen in DRPLA are mainly in the dentate nucleus, with its projection to the red nucleus, and the external pallidum, with its projection to the subthalamic nucleus. The dentatorubral system (dentate nucleus to the red nucleus) is usually more affected. Atrophies in basal ganglia nuclei, the thalamus, and the inferior olives can also be seen [11] [11].

Degenerative lesions have been found in the cerebral white matter, putamen, Goll's nucleus of the medulla oblongata, and lateral corticospinal and Goll's tract of the spinal cord [12][12].

Signs and symptoms

DRPLA presented clinical features are presented ahead. Main disease features/constant clinical findings, with symptoms found in patients with early onset and later onset:

Main findings
  • Myoclonic epilepsy
  • Dementia (progressive)
  • Ataxia
  • Choreoathetosis
  • Cerebellar Ataxia
  • Dysarthria
Early onset
  • Myoclonus epilepsy
  • Opsoclonus
Later onset
  • Choreic or dystonic movements
  • Personality changes
  • Hallucinations
  • Delusional ideas
  • Oculomotor disturbances
